1.1 — Test Structure & Scoring

Foundation▼

Theory & Explanation

Test Sections & Weights The STEP has 100 questions in total, divided across 4 sections:

① Reading Comprehension (RC) — 40 questions, 40% of score, 75 minutes
② Sentence Structure / Grammar (ST) — 30 questions, 30% of score
③ Listening Comprehension (LC) — 20 questions, 20% of score
④ Compositional Analysis (CA) — 10 questions, 10% of score, 30 minutes

Total time: 3 hours (including trial questions)
CEFR Score Levels Scores are 0–100 and map to CEFR levels:
A1: 0–30 (Foundation) | A2: 31–45 (Beginner) | B1: 46–60 (Intermediate)
B2: 61–75 (Above Average) | C1: 76–90 (Advanced) | C2: 91–100 (Proficient)

Most universities require 60–75. Some programs require 75+.
💡 Teacher Tip: Ask the student what score they need and work backward. A student needing B2 (61–75) must focus especially on Reading (40%) and Grammar (30%) since those two sections alone represent 70% of the total.

1.2 — Reading Strategies: Skimming & Scanning

Core Strategy▼

Theory & Explanation

Skimming (General Understanding) Read quickly to get the main idea — do NOT read every word. Focus on:
• First sentence of each paragraph (topic sentence)
• Any bolded words (there will be a question about them)
• The last paragraph (usually a conclusion or main point)
Scanning (Finding Details) Look for a specific word, number, or name — like searching in a list. Use the questions as a map. Since questions follow the passage in order, question 1 = paragraph 1, question 2 = paragraph 2, etc.
Proven Approach for STEP Reading ① Skim the passage once (30 seconds)
② Read question 1, scan paragraph 1 for the answer
③ Mark the answer, move to question 2 and paragraph 2
④ Skip hard questions — come back after finishing easy ones
⑤ Main idea / best title question: answer it LAST
💡 Teacher Tip: Remind students: the passage has NO title on the real exam. Paragraphs are numbered [1], [2], [3]… A word in bold guarantees a vocabulary question.

1.3 — The Elimination Strategy

Test-Taking Skill▼

Theory & Explanation

Process of Elimination (POE) When unsure, remove obviously wrong answers first:
• Is it directly contradicted by the passage? → Eliminate
• Is it too extreme? (always, never, all, none) → Usually eliminate
• Is it true in general but NOT mentioned in the passage? → Eliminate
• Is it only partially correct? → Eliminate

If you can eliminate 2 options, your chance of guessing correctly goes from 25% to 50%.
💡 Trick: The correct reading answer is almost always directly supported by specific words in the passage. If you can't point to the sentence that proves your answer — it's probably wrong.

2.1 — How Context Clues Work

Core Skill▼

Theory & Explanation

Type 1 — Definition Clue The author defines the word in the same sentence.
Example: "The patient showed lethargy, or extreme tiredness and lack of energy."
→ "lethargy" = extreme tiredness
Type 2 — Synonym Clue A word nearby has the same meaning.
Example: "The explorer was intrepid, brave enough to cross the desert alone."
→ "intrepid" ≈ brave
Type 3 — Contrast Clue (antonym signal words: but, however, although, unlike) A contrasting word or phrase appears nearby.
Example: "Although she was usually taciturn, she talked non-stop at the party."
→ "taciturn" = quiet/silent (contrast with "talked non-stop")
Type 4 — Example Clue Examples given nearby explain the word.
Example: "He collected vintage items — a 1940s radio, an antique clock, and old coins."
→ "vintage" = old, from a past era
💡 KEY RULE: A word can have many meanings. Always choose the meaning that fits THIS passage's context, not the most common everyday meaning. The word "star" in an astronomy passage ≠ "star" in a movie passage.

Session 3 — Reading: Main Idea, Supporting Details & General Understanding

3.1 — Main Idea vs. Topic vs. Supporting Detail

Core Distinction▼

Theory & Explanation

Topic — The broad subject (1–3 words). "Renewable energy"
Main Idea — What the author says ABOUT the topic (a full idea). "Renewable energy is a promising but challenging solution to the world's energy needs."
Supporting Detail — A specific fact, example, or reason that proves the main idea. "Solar panels cost more than traditional energy sources."
How to spot main idea questions:
• "The passage is mainly about…"
• "What is the best title for this passage?"
• "What is the primary purpose of this passage?"
• "Which statement best expresses the main idea?"

Rule: The correct main idea answer must cover ALL paragraphs — not just one. If an answer only describes one paragraph, eliminate it.
💡 Teacher Tip: Main idea answers are always the LAST question for a passage on the STEP. Train students to skip it and come back after reading the whole passage.

4.1 — Inference vs. Detail: Know the Difference

Core Distinction▼

Theory & Explanation

Detail Question → The answer is written IN the passage. You just scan and find it.
Signal words: "According to the passage…", "The passage states…", "What does paragraph [2] say about…"
Inference Question → The answer is NOT directly written. You must THINK beyond the text using logic + what the passage implies.
Signal words: "It can be inferred that…", "The author implies…", "What would most likely happen…", "What can be concluded…", "The passage suggests…"
Tone / Purpose Questions
• Informative: presents facts, research, statistics
• Persuasive: argues a position, uses words like "must," "should," "it is essential"
• Critical: challenges or questions something
• Descriptive: paints a picture, heavy on adjectives
• Advisory: gives recommendations — "researchers recommend…"
💡 Teacher Tip: Inference answers must be SUPPORTED by the passage — they're just not stated directly. If an answer requires outside knowledge not hinted at in the passage, it's wrong. The "too far" answer is a common trap.

5.1 — Simple Tenses + Time Signals

Foundation▼

Theory & Explanation

Simple Present — habits, facts, schedules
Form: base verb (+ s/es for he/she/it)
Time signals: always, usually, often, sometimes, every day, never, rarely, on Mondays
Example: "She works at the hospital every weekend."
Simple Past — completed action at a specific past time
Form: verb + ed (or irregular form)
Time signals: yesterday, last week, last year, in 2020, ago, when I was young
Example: "He visited Paris last summer."
Simple Future — planned or predicted future actions
Form: will + base verb / am going to + base verb
Time signals: tomorrow, next week, soon, in the future, later
Example: "They will launch the new product next month."
💡 Exam Tip: On the STEP, always look for the TIME SIGNAL first — it tells you which tense to use. The time signal is the most reliable clue.

5.2 — Perfect & Continuous Tenses

★★★ Heavy on Exam▼

Theory & Explanation

Present Perfect — past action with present relevance, or unspecified time
Form: have/has + past participle
Signals: just, already, yet, ever, never, recently, so far, since, for
Example: "I have never visited Japan." / "She has worked here since 2018."
Past Perfect — action completed BEFORE another past action
Form: had + past participle
Signal: "By the time…", "Before…", "After… had…", "When … had…"
Example: "By the time they arrived, the meeting had already ended."
Present Perfect Continuous — action that started in the past and is still happening
Form: have/has + been + verb-ing
Signals: since, for, all day, lately, recently
Example: "He has been studying for three hours." / "She has been working here since 2015."
Past Continuous — ongoing action interrupted by another past action
Form: was/were + verb-ing
Signal: "when," "while," "at that moment"
Example: "I was reading when the phone rang."
✗ Wrong
"She works at this company since 2015." (needs present perfect)
✓ Correct
"She has been working at this company since 2015."

Three interconnected grammar areas that regularly appear on STEP — and are often confused by students.

⏱ 90 minGrammar — 30%★★★ High Frequency

6.1 — Modal Verbs

★★ Frequently Tested▼

Theory & Explanation

Modal Verb Rules
• Modal + base verb (no -s, no -ing, no -ed): "She can swim." ✓ (NOT "She can swims.")
• Negation: modal + not + base verb: "He should not eat junk food."

Meanings:
can = ability/permission (present) | could = ability (past) / polite request
will = certain future | would = hypothetical / polite
should = advice / recommendation | must = obligation or certainty
may / might = possibility | have to = external obligation
must not = prohibition | do not have to = no obligation
✗ Wrong
"You should to rest." / "He can runs fast."
✓ Correct
"You should rest." / "He can run fast."

6.2 — Conditional Sentences

★★★ Must Know▼

Theory & Explanation

Zero Conditional — Always true facts/scientific truths
If + present simple → present simple
"If you heat water to 100°C, it boils."
First Conditional — Real/possible future situations
If + present simple → will + base verb
"If she studies hard, she will pass the exam."
Second Conditional — Unreal/imaginary present situations
If + past simple → would + base verb
"If I had more time, I would travel the world." (but I don't have time)
Third Conditional — Unreal past situations (regrets, what-ifs)
If + past perfect → would have + past participle
"If she had studied harder, she would have passed." (but she didn't study)
💡 Common STEP Trap: "If she was taller…" = WRONG (Second Conditional needs were for all subjects). Correct: "If she were taller…"

6.3 — Passive Voice

★★ Frequently Tested▼

Theory & Explanation

Formula: be (correct tense) + past participle

Simple Present: "They make coffee here." → "Coffee is made here."
Simple Past: "She wrote the report." → "The report was written by her."
Present Perfect: "They have approved the plan." → "The plan has been approved."
Future: "They will announce the results." → "The results will be announced."
Modal: "You should submit the form." → "The form should be submitted."

7.1 — Subject-Verb Agreement (SVA)

★★★ Tricky — High Freq▼

Theory & Explanation

Basic Rule: Singular subject → singular verb. Plural subject → plural verb.
"The dog barks." / "The dogs bark."
Tricky Case 1 — each / every / either / neither / one / anyone / no one
These are ALWAYS singular even if followed by "of the [plural noun]":
✓ "Each of the students is responsible." (NOT are)
✓ "Everyone has submitted their form."
✓ "Neither answer is correct."
Tricky Case 2 — Neither…nor / Either…or / Not only…but also
The verb agrees with the subject CLOSEST to it:
✓ "Neither the manager nor the employees were informed."
✓ "Either the students or the teacher is responsible."
Tricky Case 3 — Collective Nouns
Usually treated as singular in American English (STEP standard):
✓ "The team is ready." / "The committee has decided."
Tricky Case 4 — Separated Subject + Verb
A phrase between subject and verb does NOT change the verb:
✓ "The quality of the products is excellent." (subject = quality, not products)

7.2 — Articles: a / an / the / Ø

★★★ Must Know▼

Theory & Explanation

A / An — indefinite, first mention, one of many
• "a" before consonant SOUNDS: a book, a university (u = "you" sound)
• "an" before vowel SOUNDS: an apple, an honest man (h is silent!), an hour
Example: "She found a solution." / "He is an expert."
The — definite, specific, both speaker & listener know which one
USE "the" before:
• Something mentioned again: "I saw a dog. The dog was friendly."
• Unique things: the sun, the moon, the internet, the president
• Superlatives: the fastest, the most important
• Rivers, oceans, deserts, mountain ranges: the Nile, the Pacific, the Sahara
• Ordinals: the first, the second chapter
No article (Ø) — general plural / uncountable, proper nouns
• Ø countries, cities, languages: Ø Saudi Arabia, Ø Riyadh, Ø Arabic
• Ø general plural: Ø Books are important. / Ø Water is essential.
• BUT: the USA, the UAE, the Netherlands (country names with "of/states/republic")

7.3 — Prepositions of Time & Place

★★ Frequently Tested▼

Theory & Explanation

Time Prepositions

8.1 — Gerunds vs. Infinitives

★★★ Very High Frequency▼

Theory & Explanation

Verbs + GERUND (-ing) — SAFE: enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, recommend, admit, deny, consider, practice, keep, mind, risk, miss, stop, quit
Memory trick: "SAFE" = Stop, Avoid, Finish, Enjoy
✓ "She enjoys reading." / "He avoided making eye contact." / "They finished writing the report."
Verbs + INFINITIVE (to + base) — WANT: want, need, hope, plan, decide, agree, refuse, expect, promise, offer, manage, fail, choose, tend
✓ "She decided to leave." / "He agreed to help." / "They plan to open a new branch."
Verbs that take BOTH (with different meanings)
• stop + -ing = quit doing it: "He stopped smoking." (quit)
• stop + to = pause in order to: "He stopped to smoke." (paused for a smoke break)
• remember + -ing = recall a past event: "I remember meeting him."
• remember + to = don't forget to do it: "Remember to lock the door."

8.2 — Adjectives, Adverbs & Comparatives

★★ Frequently Tested▼

Theory & Explanation

Adjective vs. Adverb
• Adjective → describes a NOUN: "She is a careful driver." / "The music was loud."
• Adverb → describes a VERB, adjective, or another adverb: "She drives carefully." / "It was extremely loud."

After linking verbs (be, seem, feel, look, smell, taste, sound) → use ADJECTIVE:
✓ "She looks tired." (NOT "She looks tiredly.")
Comparatives & Superlatives
Short words (1 syllable): add -er / -est: fast → faster / fastest
Long words (2+ syllables): use more / most: careful → more careful / most careful
Irregular: good → better → best | bad → worse → worst | far → farther → farthest

✓ "Kamal is the tallest of his four brothers." (From official Qiyas guide)
Common trap: "He is more taller" ← WRONG (double comparative)

8.3 — Subjunctive Mood

★★ STEP Favourite▼

Theory & Explanation

Subjunctive = base verb (no -s, no tense change) after demand/suggest/recommend/insist/require/propose + that

✓ "The doctor recommended that he rest for a week." (NOT rests / rested)
✓ "I suggest that she study harder." (NOT studies / studied)
✓ "The committee insists that every member be on time." (NOT is / was)
💡 Exam Tip: On STEP, if you see "recommend/suggest/insist/require/demand + that + [subject]," the verb that follows is always the BASE FORM regardless of the subject (he, she, it, they).

10.1 — Punctuation Rules

★★★ Must Know▼

Theory & Explanation

COMMA — 5 Key Uses
① After introductory clause: "Although it rained, , we went out."
② Before coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so): "She studied, but she failed."
③ Between items in a list: "We need eggs, flour, and sugar."
④ Around non-essential information: "My brother, who lives in Jeddah, called me."
⑤ After transitional phrases: "However, , the results were unexpected."
SEMICOLON — 2 Uses
① Between two independent clauses (without a conjunction): "Life is short; art is long."
② Between items in a complex list: "The team included Ali, a manager; Sara, a designer; and Khalid, a developer."

❌ WRONG: "She wanted to go; but it was late." (Don't use semicolon WITH a conjunction)
COLON — 2 Uses
① To introduce a list: "The report covers three areas: finance, marketing, and operations."
② To introduce an explanation: "The reason was clear: they had run out of funding."
APOSTROPHE — 2 Uses
① Possession: "the student's book" / "the students' books" (plural)
② Contraction: "it's = it is" | "don't = do not" | Note: "its" (no apostrophe) = belonging to it

10.2 — Capitalization Rules

★★ Frequently Tested▼

Theory & Explanation

Always Capitalize
✓ First word of every sentence
✓ Proper nouns: names of people (Ahmed), cities (Riyadh), countries (Saudi Arabia), companies (Aramco)
✓ Languages & nationalities: Arabic, English, Saudi, American
✓ Specific titles BEFORE a name: Professor Hassan, King Salman, Dr. Ahmed
✓ Days, months: Monday, March (NOT seasons: summer, winter)
✓ Named institutions: King Abdulaziz University, Ministry of Education
Do NOT Capitalize
❌ Seasons: summer, winter, spring, autumn
❌ General titles not before a name: "I visited my doctor." (but "I visited Dr. Hassan.")
❌ Common nouns: "I went to a university." (but "I studied at King Saud University.")

10.3 — Signal Words for Sentence Ordering

★★★ High Frequency▼

Theory & Explanation

Signal Words Show the Relationship Between Sentences

Contrast: but, however, although, even though, yet, despite, on the other hand, nevertheless
Addition: and, also, moreover, furthermore, in addition, as well as
Cause/Effect: because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, so
Sequence/Time: first, then, next, after, finally, subsequently, later
Conclusion: in conclusion, to summarize, ultimately, in short
Example: for example, for instance, such as, including
Ordering Strategy:
① Find the TOPIC SENTENCE (introduces the main idea — usually the first)
② Find CONCLUSION signals (as a result, therefore, finally → usually the last)
③ Find CONNECTORS that link sentences (this, these, it → refers back to a previous idea)
④ Arrange: Introduction → Development/Details → Conclusion

🔁 B — If-Clauses / Conditionals (Repeated Every Year)

تسريبات ▼

Quick Reference

Type 0 (Always True) — If + present simple, present simple: If you heat ice, it melts.
Type 1 (Real Future) — If + present simple, will + base: If it rains, we will stay home.
Type 2 (Unreal Present) — If + past simple, would + base: If I had a car, I would drive to work.
Type 3 (Impossible Past) — If + past perfect, would have + past participle: If she had studied, she would have passed.

Passive Formula & Tense Sequence

Passive Voice = be + past participle (+ by + agent optional): The report was written by the director.
Tense in Passive — Present simple: is/are + pp | Past simple: was/were + pp | Perfect: has/have been + pp | Future: will be + pp | Continuous: is being + pp
Sequence of Tenses — After past reporting verbs (said, told, thought), shift the tense: She said she "was leaving" (not "is leaving").
